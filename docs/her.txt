Relevant Historical Background


33.   The Site lies within an area which remained rural until Oxford Road was
      laid out in the 1790s. The Laurent map of 1793 (not included in the
      statement) shows the main axis of Oxford Road and the early street
      network which is traceable today. This urban layout of the earliest origin is
      contained within the eastern section of Whitworth Street West (originally
      named    Gloucester   Street),   now   designated    as   Whitworth   Street
      Conservation Area, but the western arm, which contains the Site was
      largely undeveloped. The River Medlock meandered through fields, with
      the only group of buildings near the location of the later Tib Lock,
      assessed in this report. The Rochdale Canal opened in 1800 being crucial
      to the area’s development as a ‘rapidly advancing frontier of Manchester’s
                                                                                      Figure 3: 1848 OS Map, 6 inch, Lancashire CIV (includes: Manchester; Salford.)
      industrialisation’.

34.   By the 1820s the area became home to a number of textile mills, iron
      foundries, food processing factories, slaughterhouses, coal and timber
      yards. The Ordinance Survey (OS) Map of 1848 ( Figure 3) shows the area
      fully developed, with the section to the north of Whitworth Street West
      between the junction with Gloucester Street and the Albion Street junction
      occupied by dye works and mills along the canalside and the area west of
      Albion Street being a dense area of housing. At this time gas works
      occupied the site which was later developed as Oxford Road station, with
      coal wharf located to the north of Gloucester Street, later renamed
      Whitworth Street West.

35.   Major changes in the area, which weren ’t reflected on the 1848 OS Map
      were centred around the construction of the railway, opened in 1849. The
      arrival of Manchester South Junction (MSJ) & Altrincham Railway Company
      (AR) is shown in 1896 OS Map (Figure 4); the railways had a significant
      impact on the layout and character of the area, which became more urban

      The Manchester South Junction & Altrincham Railway viaduct was constructed in
      1846-9 by David Bellhouse junior for the Manchester South Junction & Altrincham
      Railway (MSJ&AR) Company; Bellhouse was also responsible for laying the track.
      Several of the cast-iron bridges situated along the viaduct were built by his son, the
      engineer and iron-founder, Edward Taylor Bellhouse, along with others by the line's
      Chief Engineer, William Baker, and William Cubitt.
      The MSJ&AR line is one of the country's earliest suburban railways and was the first
      in Manchester.[…]
      300,000 cubic feet of stone (91,440 cubic metres), 50 million bricks and 3000 tons
      (3048 tonnes) of cast iron were used in the viaduct's construction. The MSJ&AR line
      opened on 20 July 1849, although sections of the viaduct were not opened until 1
      August 1849. The line ran for 8 1/2 miles from London Road Station via Oxford
      Road and Deansgate Stations to Altrincham, with the viaduct forming a 1 ¾ mile (2
      ¾ km) long stretch: the northern branch terminating at the north-west bank of the
      River Irwell, and the southern branch terminating at the east side of Dawson Street.


39.   The architectural interest of the viaduct derives from its relatively early
      construction achieved by the combination of the genius of Victorian
      engineering and architecture where pride in this technical achievement
      was illustrated in the attention to detail and aesthetic quality of materials
      and forms. Some arches have been filled in and the brick parapet altered
      and rebuilt in places; some later repairs have been carried out to some of
      the arches, according to the list description. The only part of the viaduct
      which was inspected during the site visit was the nearest stretch to the

40.   The viaduct consists of a series of predominantly arched, cast -iron bridges
      by Edward Taylor Bellhouse, William Baker and William Cubitt that cross
      various streets, the Rochdale Canal, and the Bridgewater Canal. Some of

      detailing and castellated towers (referencing the location of a Roman fort
      at Castlefield), whilst others, such as the bridge over Gloucester Street,
      located in close vicinity of the Site, is more classically detailed.

      a grand and considerable presence at street level.

The viaduct incorporates a series of predominantly arched, cast-iron bridges by Edward Taylor Bell-
house, William Baker and William Cubitt that cross various streets, the Rochdale Canal, and the
Bridgewater Canal. The bridges have an average span of 70ft, although one of Baker's bridges, which
spans the Rochdale Canal has a span of 105ft. A number of the bridges incorporate highly decorative
Gothic-style ironwork and detailing and castellated towers (referencing the location of a Roman fort
at Castlefield), whilst others, such as a bridge over Gloucester Street are more classically detailed. A
bridge over Egerton Street by William Baker was replaced in steel in 1976. Some of the viaduct's
brick arches and bridges are skewed. The Irwell Bridge at the north-west end of the viaduct is of
brick with two segmental arches, each of approximately 65ft span, with a central pier and cut-water.
Rusticated stone voussoirs and pilaster strips exist to the central pier and abutments, and the para-
pet above the western arch incorporates cast-iron panelling.

Following electrification of the line in the C20 the viaduct now incorporates a series of C20 and early-
C21 gantries along its course. Also along the viaduct's course are a number of attached or abutting
buildings, most of which are later in date and are un-related to the viaduct's use; all are excluded
from the listing, which relates solely to the viaduct. Manchester Piccadilly Station, Deansgate Station
and Manchester Oxford Road Station (the two latter stations being constructed on top of and to the
side of the viaduct) are all separately listed at Grade II.
